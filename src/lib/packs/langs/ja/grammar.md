# Particles and sentence shape

Japanese marks the job of a phrase with a short particle placed **after** it.
Word order can therefore move more freely than in English, but the predicate —
a verb, adjective, or noun plus です — normally comes last.

`私(わたし)は毎朝(まいあさ)コーヒーを飲(の)みます。` means “I drink coffee
every morning.” Here, `は` marks what the sentence is about, `を` marks what is
drunk, and `飲みます` finishes the sentence.

## The topic は and the subject が

`は` is written *ha* but pronounced **wa** when it is a particle. It sets the
topic or creates a contrast: “as for …”. `が` marks the grammatical subject and
often identifies new, noticed, or specifically requested information.

- `私は学生(がくせい)です。` — As for me, I am a student.
- `だれが来(き)ますか。田中(たなか)さんが来ます。` — Who will come?
  Tanaka will.
- `猫(ねこ)は好きです。犬(いぬ)は苦手(にがて)です。` — I like cats; dogs,
  on the other hand, are not my strength.

Do not force a one-to-one English translation. The useful beginner question is:
am I establishing or contrasting a topic (`は`), or identifying the subject
that answers “who?” or “what?” (`が`)? Japanese often omits a topic already clear
from context, so `学生です` can be a complete answer to “What do you do?”.

## The object を

`を` is pronounced **o** and marks the direct object of an action.

- `本(ほん)を読(よ)みます。` — I read a book.
- `音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)きます。` — I listen to music.

Some movement verbs also use `を` for the space passed through or left:
`公園(こうえん)を歩(ある)きます` (walk through the park) and
`電車(でんしゃ)を降(お)ります` (get off the train).

## Location and direction: に, で, and へ

These particles are easy to confuse because English often translates all three
with “at”, “in”, or “to”. Look at the role of the place instead.

- `に` marks a destination, a point in time, a recipient, or the location where
  something exists: `学校(がっこう)に行(い)きます` (go to school),
  `七時(しちじ)に起(お)きます` (wake at seven),
  `友達(ともだち)に本を渡(わた)します` (give a book to a friend), and
  `公園に猫がいます` (there is a cat in the park).
- `で` marks where an action happens or the means used:
  `公園で走(はし)ります` (run in the park) and
  `電車で行きます` (go by train).
- `へ`, pronounced **e**, emphasizes direction rather than the arrival point:
  `日本(にほん)へ行きます` (head to Japan). With many movement verbs, `に` and
  `へ` are both possible, with this small difference in focus.

A practical contrast is `図書館(としょかん)にいます` (be at the library) versus
`図書館で勉強(べんきょう)します` (study at the library).

## Useful connectors: の, と, も, から, and まで

- `の` links nouns, often for possession or description:
  `先生(せんせい)の本` (the teacher’s book),
  `日本語(にほんご)の授業(じゅぎょう)` (Japanese class).
- `と` joins a complete list or marks a companion or quotation:
  `パンと卵(たまご)` (bread and eggs), `友達と話します` (talk with a friend),
  `「はい」と言(い)いました` (said “yes”).
- `も` means “also” or “too” and often takes the position of `は`, `が`, or `を`:
  `私も行きます` (I will go too).
- `から` marks a starting point and `まで` an endpoint:
  `九時(くじ)から五時(ごじ)まで` (from nine until five).

## Questions and conversational omission

Add `か` to the end of a polite sentence to make a neutral question:
`日本語を話しますか。` In casual conversation, speakers often omit `か` and use
rising intonation, but the polite form is the safer default for this pack.

Conversation also drops particles when the relationship is obvious:
`これ、食(た)べる？` instead of `これを食べますか。` This is natural, not
careless.
For a beginner, keep particles while learning the structure, then notice how
native speech omits especially `は`, `が`, and `を`. Particles such as `に` and
`で` carry distinctions that are harder to recover, so dropping them is less
generally safe.

## A reliable way to build a sentence

1. Choose the final predicate: `行きます`, `おいしいです`, `学生です`.
2. Add the phrases it needs and mark each role with a particle.
3. Use `は` to establish familiar context and `が` when the subject itself is
   the information being identified.
4. Remove a repeated topic only when the listener can recover it easily.

Japanese does not require every English subject or pronoun to appear. A natural
sentence gives the listener enough context; it does not repeat information just
to fill an English-shaped slot.
